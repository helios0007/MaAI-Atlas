# Presentation practice: assertion-evidence slides, spoken script, and avoiding "AI slop" design

Scope: a short, practical checklist to rebuild an HTML slide deck for a student research review at IAAC Barcelona. The slides show only headings and visuals. The explanation goes in a spoken script.

## 1. Assertion-evidence (AE) slide structure (Michael Alley, Penn State)

### Takeaway
An AE slide has a one-sentence claim as the headline (the "assertion") and a visual as the support (the "evidence"). It has no bullet lists. Small controlled studies by Alley and Garner found better comprehension of complex ideas and better delayed recall than topic-phrase plus bullet slides. One replication found only a non-significant trend. This structure matches the presenter's "headings and visuals only" plan, if each heading is a full-sentence claim and not a topic label.

### Cited Findings
- AE replaces the default topic-phrase headline with a full-sentence assertion that states the slide's takeaway, supported by visual evidence (images, graphs, diagrams) instead of bullets. The site says phrase headlines do not communicate the takeaway and bullets make it hard for the audience to know where to look while the presenter speaks. — [assertion-evidence.com](https://www.assertion-evidence.com/)
- Garner, Alley, Wolfe and Sawarynski (2013), International Journal of Engineering Education 29(6): two audiences heard the same talk with different slides. The AE audience understood and remembered the content better (p < .01). — [Penn State Engineering Writing: Research](https://writing.engr.psu.edu/research.html); [IJEE PDF](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf)
- The same 2013 paper (110 engineering students, as summarized in search results) reports better essay comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and stronger recall at a delayed post-test for the AE group. — [IJEE PDF](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf) (sample size from search summary; I could not extract the PDF text to verify)
- ASEE 2011 (6-minute MRI talk): AE students scored higher on complex-concept questions and remembered key ideas better one week later. Topic-subtopic students did better on simple facts directly after the talk. — [ASEE PEER: AE slides appear to lead to better comprehension](https://peer.asee.org/assertion-evidence-slides-appear-to-lead-to-better-comprehension-and-recall-of-more-complex-concepts.pdf)
- Caveat: a 2012 follow-up (Wolfe, Alley, Garner) found only a positive, non-significant trend. The topic-subtopic group did much better than before, which suggests high-quality visuals help in either format. — [Penn State Pure: high-quality visual evidence may offset...](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/high-quality-visual-evidence-on-presentation-slides-may-offset-th-2/)
- Student presenters who built AE slides understood their own content better than those who built topic-subtopic slides (Aippersbach, Alley, Garner, 2013). — [Penn State Engineering Writing: Research](https://writing.engr.psu.edu/research.html); [ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/how-slide-design-affects-a-student-presenter-s-understanding-of-the-content)
- Earlier work: Alley and Neeley (2005) introduced AE; Alley et al. (2006) "How the design of headlines in presentation slides affects audience retention," Technical Communication 53(2). — [Penn State Engineering Writing: Research](https://writing.engr.psu.edu/research.html)
- Penn State also proposes "projected words per minute" (on-screen words the audience must read while the speaker talks) as a metric of slide effectiveness, to use together with words per slide. — [ASEE PEER 2010 PDF](https://peer.asee.org/projected-words-per-minute-a-window-into-the-potential-effectiveness-of-presentation-slides.pdf)

### Inferences
- For this deck: write each heading as one sentence claim, max about two lines (for example "Most mapped AI projects cluster in three cities", not "Geography"). Put the explanation in the script, not on the slide.
- The 2012 caveat says the visual quality carries much of the benefit. Spend effort on real diagrams, maps, and drawings, not on decorative layout.

### Gaps
- I could not get the exact retention percentage from Alley et al. (2006); the full text was not reachable.
- All AE studies are small and come mostly from the same research group; no independent large replication found.
- assertion-evidence.com itself cites no studies on its home page; the evidence is on the Penn State research page.

## 2. Mayer's multimedia principles relevant to slides

### Takeaway
Coherence, signaling, redundancy, and segmenting all aim to cut extraneous mental load. For a live talk: remove decoration, highlight what matters, do not print what you say, and split complex ideas into steps.

### Cited Findings
- Coherence: people learn better when extraneous material is excluded. Supported in 18 of 19 tests, median effect size 0.86. — [Mayer, research-based principles (UNH PDF)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning/F29A19FCD34C542806F736E0661C05F5)
- Signaling: people learn better when cues highlight the organization of essential material. 26 of 28 tests, effect size 0.70. — [UNH PDF](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning/F29A19FCD34C542806F736E0661C05F5)
- Redundancy: people learn better from graphics plus spoken words than from graphics plus spoken words plus the same printed text. 8 of 12 tests, small effect size 0.10 in Mayer's table. — [UNH PDF](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning/F29A19FCD34C542806F736E0661C05F5)
- Segmenting: people learn better when a complex message is presented in learner-paced segments rather than as one continuous flow. — [Dartmouth: Mayer's 12 principles](https://services.dartmouth.edu/TDClient/1806/Portal/KB/PrintArticle?ID=171655); [NUS MediXperience](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/medixperience/scholarly-article/mayers-12-principles-of-multimedia-instruction/)
- Source count of principles varies (12 in Mayer 2008/2009; Ohio State lists 15). — [OSU Multimedia Learning](https://u.osu.edu/multimedialearning/?p=11)

### Inferences
- Coherence: remove decorative icons, background effects, and logos that carry no information.
- Signaling: use one accent color or an outline to point at the part of the image you are talking about; the assertion headline is also a signal.
- Redundancy: supports "headings and visuals only" with the explanation spoken.
- Segmenting: for a complex diagram, build it in 2 to 3 reveal steps or split it across slides.

### Gaps
- Segmenting evidence is mostly from learner-paced recorded lessons; in a live talk the presenter controls the pace, so the transfer is an inference.

## 3. How to write the speaker script

### Takeaway
Plan about 130 to 150 spoken words per minute and about one minute per slide, with less text on slides that show a complex visual. Memorize the main points and transitions; rehearse aloud and time it.

### Cited Findings
- Typical presentation rate is 130 to 150 wpm; a 3-minute talk is about 390 to 450 words, a 5-minute talk about 650 to 750 words. — [Plus AI: words per minute](https://plusai.com/blog/words-per-minute-for-speeches-and-presentations); [SlideModel](https://slidemodel.com/what-is-a-good-words-per-minute-for-speeches/)
- Microsoft PowerPoint Speaker Coach recommends 100 to 165 wpm as the range audiences find easiest to follow. — [Microsoft Support: Speaker Coach](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/PowerPoint/suggestions-from-speaker-coach)
- Rule of thumb: about one slide per minute; subtract about 30 to 50 words for each complex visual so the audience has silent time to look. — [24Slides: speech timing](https://24slides.com/presentbetter/speech-timing-words-per-minute) (practitioner blog, rule of thumb only)
- Example architecture-review structure (about 11 min): program and design problem 1 min, approach and method 4 min, walk through drawings tied to the concept 4 min, recap 2 min. — [Archinect: The Architecture Student's Presentation Roadmap](https://www.archinect.com/features/article/150145350/the-architecture-student-s-presentation-roadmap)
- "When rehearsing, memorize big points instead of a long script." Record yourself; pause instead of saying "umm"; use your hands to guide the audience to the drawing. — [Archinect](https://www.archinect.com/features/article/150145350/the-architecture-student-s-presentation-roadmap)
- Practising aloud is the step students most often skip. — [StudioMatrx jury guide](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/studio-jury-survival-architecture-students-india)

### Inferences
- Per-slide script pattern (derived from AE plus signaling): (1) say the assertion in plain words; (2) point to the evidence ("on the left, the red dots are..."); (3) give one implication; (4) a one-sentence transition that asks the question the next slide answers.
- Keep the script in short spoken sentences; it is a speaking aid, not a reading text.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed source on per-slide script structure or transition wording; the pattern above is an inference.

## 4. Signs of generic AI-generated slide and web design, and what to do instead

### Takeaway
Community sources (2024 to 2026) agree on a set of "AI default" tells: purple-to-blue gradients, gradient headline text, glass cards everywhere, glows, three equal cards in a row, centered stacked hero, Inter everywhere, emoji or stock icons on every item. In text: em dashes, "not X but Y" constructions, rule-of-three lists, puffery. The fix: fewer components, one accent, editorial type, real images and data. These are opinion and practice sources, not measured research.

### Cited Findings
Visual tells and replacements (from an "anti AI slop" ban list):
- Default purple-to-blue gradient backgrounds -> solid colors or image-based hero. — [Anti AI slop ban list (GitHub wiki)](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Gradient text on headlines -> single-color headlines; gradient at most on one element. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Glassmorphism on every panel -> at most one translucent element, only where it means something. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Glow on every card or button, neon glow on text, animated rainbow borders -> no glow, at most one accent. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Three equal cards in a row -> asymmetric layout (one large, two small). Centered stacked hero -> left-aligned editorial layout or 60/40 split. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Decorative abstract imagery -> real photo, screenshot, or image that carries meaning. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Inter for both display and body -> a distinctive display face paired with a body face. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Low-contrast pastel accents -> meet WCAG AA 4.5:1 for text. — [same](https://github.com/Laith0003/ux-skill/wiki/Anti-AI-slop-ban-list)
- Commentators describe the recurring template: purple-blue gradient, centered hero, three feature cards with generic icons, default fonts. — [Built In: AI makes bad design look good enough](https://builtin.com/articles/ai-design-slop-era); [DEV: why every AI-generated template looks the same](https://dev.to/binbreeze/why-every-ai-generated-template-looks-the-same-and-how-im-fighting-it-3lo2); [Kosta Canatselis: Spot the slop](https://world.hey.com/kostac/spot-the-slop-a-ui-designer-s-guide-to-fixing-ai-defaults-4c448c9c)
- Emoji used everywhere is named as a tell. — [Product Hunt: Uiverse Design](https://www.producthunt.com/p/uiverse-io/uiverse-design)

Writing tells (Wikipedia guide by WikiProject AI Cleanup):
- "Negative parallelisms": "Not just X, but also Y", "Not X, but Y", "Y rather than X", which seem to correct a misconception nobody stated. — [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
- Rule of three (triplet lists); undue emphasis on significance and legacy (puffery); promotional language; excessive boldface. — [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
- Overuse of em dashes, often in places a human would use a comma, colon, or parentheses. The guide warns that no single sign is proof; signs matter in combination. — [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); [FlowingData summary](https://flowingdata.com/2025/10/20/signs-of-ai-writing-on-wikipedia/); [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/chatgpt-hypen-em-dash-ai-writing-1235314945/)

### Inferences
- Deck checklist: one background color, one accent color, no gradient text, no glass cards, no glows, no emoji, no icon-on-every-item; a consistent grid with left-aligned headings; one serif or distinctive display face plus one plain sans for labels.
- Remove "eyebrow" labels above every heading (small caps tag + big title) unless they carry real information such as a section number.
- In headings and script: no em dashes, no "not X but Y", no "groundbreaking / seamless / powerful", no automatic lists of three. Use specific numbers and names instead of superlatives.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research on "AI slop" visual signs; all sources are practitioner blogs, wikis, or tool marketing. Treat as design consensus, not evidence.
- The impeccable anti-patterns page (mintlify) returned HTTP 410 and could not be read.
- "Eyebrow labels on every slide" was not named explicitly in the sources I read; it is an inference.

## 5. Architecture-school presentation conventions

### Takeaway
Juries want to see the drawings and understand the thinking. Keep text to a short concept statement, make drawings large and readable from jury distance, and present fewer drawings well.

### Cited Findings
- Minimize text: a short concept statement, with at most a very brief explanation. — [Arch2O: 10 tips for architecture project presentation](https://www.arch2o.com/?p=168536)
- "Filling your drawings with slabs of text doesn't really work." — [Design Jury Guide (Scribd, unverified upload)](https://www.scribd.com/document/448206881/Design-Jury-Guide)
- Test boards at jury distance (about 2 to 3 m) to check if the layout reads. A disorganized board forces jurors to search instead of absorb. — [learnarchitecture.net: presentation tips](https://learnarchitecture.net/articles/34015-architecture-presentation-tips.html); [learnarchitecture.net: jury tip](https://learnarchitecture.net/articles/32828-architecture-jury-presentation-tip.html)
- "Your drawings are merely a means to communicate your ideas, they are not an end in themselves." Explaining fewer drawings well is better than showing many you cannot discuss. — [Archinect](https://www.archinect.com/features/article/150145350/the-architecture-student-s-presentation-roadmap)
- A typical jury: about 10 to 20 minutes of presentation followed by a similar-length discussion. — [learnarchitecture.net](https://learnarchitecture.net/articles/32828-architecture-jury-presentation-tip.html)
- Treat the review as a workshop, not a defense. — [Archinect](https://www.archinect.com/features/article/150145350/the-architecture-student-s-presentation-roadmap)

### Inferences
- For a screen deck: show key drawings, maps, and diagrams full-bleed or near full-bleed; keep line weights and labels large enough to read from the back of the room (apply the "jury distance" test to the projector).
- Keep one consistent grid and caption position across all slides so the jury does not have to search.

### Gaps
- I found no source specific to IAAC's review format or a written standard for full-bleed slides in architecture schools; the guidance is from general architecture-school blogs and is mostly about pin-up boards, not slide decks.
